The Four Questions — Method & Inferences

What behaviour drives more chatting and makes users come back — so we can replicate it. Answered across every subscription cohort.

Goal & how we read the data

The goal. Isolate the causal, transferable behaviours behind chatting more and coming back — separating real levers from mere markers of users who were already going to engage — so they can be engineered for the wider base (especially the ~1M renewers who barely touch chat).

Two decisions that shaped every answer

  • Depth ≠ Return. "Chats more" and "comes back" are treated as two separate outcomes throughout.
  • The engagement-propensity trap. Engaged users do more of everything, so raw correlations overstate every feature. A behaviour only counts as a lever if its effect survives a depth-controlled test: feature measured on day 1, outcome measured strictly after, compared within message-volume strata.

Definitions & sources

  • Return = chat-to-chat, any character; return-7d = crossing the day 1→day 2 "cliff."
  • Cohort assigned point-in-time as of each user's first chat day.
  • Sources: product event logs (events), the subscription-history table (cohorts), and the full chat-message store (10M messages, full text).
  • All findings are quasi-causal (observational) — the clean proof is a day-1 randomised test, not yet run.

Q1 · How do users return?

Return mechanics: the shape of the curve, which door they come through, and where the habit forms.

What we looked at

  • D0-anchored return curves (D1–D30) by cohort
  • Entry surface of each session (home rail, in-player bubble, chat tab, deeplink)
  • Across-visit continuation: P(reach visit N+1 | reached N)

How we looked at it

  • Right-censored return rates (only users with full follow-up)
  • ASOF join to attribute each session to a preceding entry click (90% coverage)
  • Conditional-retention / hazard curve to find where a habit locks in

Inference — return is one narrow, cliff-shaped funnel

91% of users never make a second day. Only ~9% cross the day-1→day-2 cliff. Those who survive keep going — continuation climbs 9% → 23% → 38% → 62% and plateaus in the high-70s by visit 5–6 (a habit locks in) — but almost nobody gets there. The first return is the whole game.

Q2 · What makes them come back?

The triggers — separating real levers from markers, in every cohort.

What we looked at

  • Candidate levers: games, selfie, emotional disclosure, content-play, groups, multi-character breadth
  • Conversation topics (romance, emotional, show-content, sexual, casual…)

How we looked at it

  • Depth-controlled day-1 test + dose-response (0 → 1 → 2+ levers), run within every cohort
  • Topic lexicon on user messages → retention by topic
  • Cross-checked on two independent datasets (events + transcripts)

Inference — three levers survive, and they lift the cliff in every cohort

Cohort0 levers1 lever2+ levers
Trial7.0%14.5%28.2%
Renewal9.3%15.6%28.7%
Free3.0%9.3%23.2%

Levers = games · selfie · emotional disclosure, adopted on day 1. One lever ≈ doubles first-return; two-plus roughly triples it — in every cohort.

Inference — the topics that retain vs. the topics that don't

The mechanism is emotional investment + a reason to return — not longer sessions or more recommendation cards. Show-content retains near-top yet is only ~2.4% of messages: the one topic that helps retention and the streaming business, badly under-used.
What does NOT bring them back: content-play (diverts to watching — a watch-time win, not chat-return), groups (~2% adoption, viral loop dead), raw session length, catalogue breadth, and character warmth-vs-aggression (no retention difference).

Q3 · What is the tipping point?

The point from which a user starts chatting more — and whether it's a lever or a mirage.

What we looked at

  • Within-session: return vs. day-1 message count (fine buckets)
  • Across-visits: continuation probability by visit number

How we looked at it

  • Changepoint search on the depth→return curve
  • Conditional-continuation (survival) curve, censored for follow-up
  • Repeated within every cohort

Inference — two very different answers

Day-1 messagesReturn-7d
13.5%
5–95.0%
10–197–8%
20–4910–14%
50–9922%
100+41%
Within a session there is no tipping point — return rises smoothly with depth. This is a marker of intent, not a lever: deep users are deep because of who they are; you can't retain someone by padding their message count. (Confirmed: a past push raised depth ~11× with no lift in return.)
The real tipping point is across visits: ~visit 5–6. Once a user chats on 5–6 distinct days, they continue ~3-in-4 times — a genuine habit. The depth→return gradient holds in every cohort (same shape, the curve just shifts down for weaker ones). But because 91% never make the first return, all the leverage sits at the day-1→day-2 cliff, not at the tipping point.

Q4 · Which feature correlates most with chat depth?

Ranking the features by their association with how much a user chats.

What we looked at

  • Depth of day-1 adopters vs. non-adopters for each feature
  • Depth by subscription cohort
  • Disclosure rate & effect split across all 7 cohorts

How we looked at it

  • Avg total messages & % deep (20+) by adoption
  • Disclosure detected via a high-precision keyword lexicon on user messages

Inference — disclosure dominates

Disclosure = the user opening up about something personal or emotional on day 1 — warmth ("aapse baat karke achha lagta hai"), loneliness ("akela"), distress ("pareshan"), affection ("pyaar"). It is by far the strongest correlate of both depth and return.

Everyone discloses at ~4–6% regardless of plan — subscription type doesn't decide whether a user opens up, and ~95% of every cohort never does (largely because they're never invited to). Depth itself is flat across cohorts (~13–15 day-1 messages, ~20% deep, including Free) — subscription type predicts return, not depth. The return-lift from disclosing is 3–5.5× in every cohort, and largest in the weakest, biggest cohorts (Free, lapsed).

The conclusion, in one place

Depth is universal; return is stratified. Everyone chats about the same amount; whether they come back is set by their cohort and by whether an emotional-investment moment happened early. The same three levers (disclosure ≫ selfie > games) and three topics (emotional, show-content, romance) drive both depth and the first-return cliff in every cohort — so the mechanism is genuinely transferable.

What to do

  • Engineer an emotional-investment moment into the first session — invite disclosure early (confidentiality framing, active listening), offer a game, prompt a selfie. This attacks the day-1→day-2 cliff, where 91% are lost. Levers are badly under-supplied (most users get zero on day 1).
  • The renewer problem is discovery, not stickiness. Paying-active users retain best — they just rarely find chat. Route them in via high-intent surfaces (the in-player bubble), not the trial-flavoured home rail.
  • Eliciting disclosure pays most on Free & lapsed users (5×+ return lift) — the largest, weakest cohorts. It is the single most transferable move.
  • Weave show-content into chat — the one topic that retains chatters and drives watching, currently ~2.4% of messages.

What to stop

  • Optimising raw session depth for retention (it's a marker, not a lever).
  • Chasing groups (dead adoption, no viral loop).
  • Treating content-recommendation cards as a retention play (they're a watch-time win — a different KPI).
Caveats. All findings are quasi-causal (observational) — the clean proof is a day-1 randomised test (offer a lever, measure the day-1→day-2 crossing). "Return" here is chat-to-chat; the platform/watch-return version (arguably the truer business metric) is built and ready to run. Topic and disclosure labels are high-precision keyword proxies, so their measured effects are floors.
